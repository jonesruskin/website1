You drop a scanned contract into a compressor. A bar fills. The file is 400 KB instead of nine
megabytes, and a green button says *Download*.

For about four seconds you are the happiest a user will ever be. You got what you came for. You
are holding a thing, and the thing is in motion. You need to send it, sign it, share it, upload
it somewhere.

The compressor has nothing to say. It has done its job. You click download, and you leave.

That moment is the last piece of free attention on the internet, and almost every tool wastes it.

## Why launches fail

The standard advice for getting a tool in front of people is to *launch*. Pick a day, write the
thread, post it on the sites where builders gather, and refresh the page for eight hours. If it
goes well you get a spike. Then the spike ends, as spikes do, and you are back to a flat line with
a slightly larger mailing list.

A launch asks strangers for attention at the moment they have the least reason to give it. They
weren't looking for your tool. They were scrolling. You are competing with everything else
that's ever been posted.

## Why directories fail

The other advice is to *list* your tool. There are directories of thousands of tools, sorted by
category, with a search box. Almost nobody starts there. Nobody wakes up wanting to browse
"Productivity, page 41". The ones with traffic sell placement, so the top of the list is
whoever paid. The rest go quiet and rot, and the lists fill up with tools that no longer exist.

Launches and directories make the same mistake. They ask for attention **before** anyone has a
reason to spend it.

## The insight

Now put yourself back in front of the compressor, holding a 400 KB PDF.

You have a reason. You are mid-task. The thing in your hands is the input to whatever comes next:
a signing tool, a form, an email. If the next tool is *right there*, one click away, you will use
it. Not because someone advertised at you, but because it is the obvious next step.

Every tool produces something, and every result is the input to someone else's tool. The
compressor's output is the signature tool's input. A transcript is the start of an article. A logo
is the start of a brand kit.

That is journey-based matching. Baton doesn't guess what you might like. It looks at what you
just made and shows **one card** for a tool that takes exactly that. One card, at the moment it
matters, not a feed and not a carousel.

## We aren't the first to notice

This isn't a new idea. It just hasn't been done for tools.

Substack says that [recommendations and its app network drive about 50% of new
subscriptions](https://substack.com/growthfeatures) on the platform. Note when those recommendations
appear: right after somebody has subscribed to something they like, at the point their
goodwill is at its highest and the next suggestion costs them one tap.

Newsletters aren't tools, and we won't pretend Substack's number will transfer. Nobody knows what
this does for a compressor or a logo maker yet. But the mechanism is the same, and tools have
nothing like it. A lot of small tools, made by one person, have to fight for every single
visitor.

## How the exchange stays fair

A network like this only works if it is symmetrical, so Baton runs on credits that move one for
one.

- When someone clicks the card **on your site**, you earn 1 credit.
- When someone arrives **at your tool** from another site's card, one credit is spent.
- Every new tool starts with 10 credits, so you can be shown before you have sent anyone.
- A tool with no credits isn't shown until it sends traffic of its own.

Then come the rules that stop it turning into a farm or an ad network. Two tools in the same
category are never paired unless both opt in, so your card won't hand your users to a rival.
Repeat clicks from the same visitor to the same tool within 24 hours don't earn credit, and a
visitor can produce at most three credited clicks on a host in a day. Every click goes through a
signed link, so credits can't be forged.

## What we won't do

We would rather say this now than be asked about it later.

- **No ads.** The card is a recommendation from a tool that fits, not a paid slot.
- **No tracking cookies.** The embed sets none. To apply the rules above we use an anonymous hash
  of the visitor that changes every day, so nobody can be followed from one day to the next.
- **No pay-to-rank on the free plan.** Paid plans can set up handshakes, which are direct pairings
  between two tools that *both* agree. You can't buy a spot on a site whose owner hasn't said
  yes.
- **No made-up proof.** This site has no logos, no testimonials and no user counts, because we
  have only just opened and we would rather be honest about it than borrow credibility.

## What happens next

The network is new, so it will be thin at first. Some early success moments will have no match,
and the card will politely stay away. That is the correct behaviour, and it is also the
reason to join early: the more tools describe what they take and what they make, the more
moments get filled.

If you make a tool, it takes about two minutes. Add one line of script, call `baton.pass()` when
your tool finishes its job, and see [what it does for makers](/makers). If you just want to get a
job done, the [trails](/trails) show step-by-step routes through free tools.

The last four seconds of your users' happiness have been going to waste. We think they can be put
to better use.
